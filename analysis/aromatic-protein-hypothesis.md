# Could an aromatic-amino-acid-rich protein produce the "lignin-like" signature?

This is the first proposal in this sequence that, if correct, would **materially help** the
foraminiferal hypothesis rather than merely survive against it. It is also testable by a method nobody
has applied to *Prototaxites*.

---

## 1. The gap it occupies is one Loron et al. concede

They do not claim to have identified the wall polymer. Verbatim:

> "It is possible that polysaccharides and proteins were **lost shortly after the organism's death or
> did not form their characteristic resistant C─O/N-rich products**."

> "There is a possibility that the molecular composition of *P. taiti* was **uniquely more liable to
> change or replacement** than that of its contemporaneous Fungi or arthropods, despite their
> occurrence in the same geological setting. Such considerations **limit our ability to pinpoint the
> exact molecular composition of the *P. taiti* cell wall**."

They also raise **melanin** — a tyrosine-derived aromatic biopolymer — as a possible component, and set
it aside not on compositional grounds but because "melanin is present across the whole tree of life,"
i.e. it is not phylogenetically diagnostic. That is a different objection from "it isn't there."

So an aromatic protein-derived residue is not excluded by the paper. It sits in the space the authors
flag as beyond their resolution.

---

## 2. Tyrosine is the stronger version of the idea

Tryptophan is the intuitive candidate — indole is the most conspicuously aromatic side chain. But
**tyrosine** fits the actual evidence far better, for a specific reason.

**Abbott et al.'s alkaline oxidation of *Prototaxites* yielded lignin monomers dominated by H-type**
(p-hydroxyphenyl), with G (guaiacyl) and S (syringyl) subordinate. Loron et al. endorse this result.

H-dominance is **anomalous for lignin**. Plant lignins are G-dominated (gymnosperm) or G+S
(angiosperm); H-units are a minor grass/compression-wood component.

But **tyrosine *is* p-hydroxyphenylalanine** — its side chain is exactly the p-hydroxyphenyl group.
Alkaline/CuO oxidation of a tyrosine-rich protein yields p-hydroxybenzaldehyde and p-hydroxybenzoic
acid: the same products scored as H-type lignin monomers.

**So the single most anomalous feature of the *Prototaxites* "lignin-like" signature is precisely what
a tyrosine-rich protein would produce.** That is a real point, and it is the strongest form of the
proposal.

Two supporting considerations:

- **Di-tyrosine cross-linking** is the standard mechanism for insoluble structural proteins — resilin,
  sclerotin, mussel byssus, plant extensins. Oxidative conditions promote di-tyrosine formation. Such
  polymers are aromatic, highly cross-linked, insoluble and diagenetically resistant: a credible way to
  build a rigid wall.
- **Melanin** is tyrosine-derived, aromatic and among the most resistant biopolymers known.

> **Caveat on sourcing:** that p-hydroxyphenyl yields are confounded between lignin and protein-derived
> tyrosine is a chemically motivated inference here, not a claim I have traced to a specific paper. The
> underlying chemistry is standard — pHBA has recognised non-lignin sources, and H-units are the least
> diagnostic lignin class — but it should be verified against the CuO-oxidation literature before being
> relied on.

## 3. Why this would matter for H3′

If the *P. taiti* wall was a cross-linked structural **protein** rather than a polyphenol, the chain of
reasoning that has been damaging the foraminiferal hypothesis changes shape:

- The discriminating observation becomes an absence of **sugars**, not an absence of **protein**.
- An allogromiid test is **>60% protein plus ~13% sugars**. A wall that is protein-rich and
  sugar-poor is far closer to that than "extinct polyphenolic biomacromolecule" is.
- Sugars are more labile than heavily cross-linked protein, so preferential loss of the 13% is
  taphonomically ordinary.

This does not rescue H3′ — the morphological and size objections in
[`allogromiid-hypothesis.md`](allogromiid-hypothesis.md) are untouched, and so are the xenophyophore
falsifiers. But it would remove the chemical objection that has been doing most of the work, which is
more than any previous move in this sequence achieved.

---

## 3b. The parsimony argument, and why it is stronger than "not excluded"

The decisive consideration is not spectroscopic but **biosynthetic**, and it favours the protein reading
substantially.

**What a polyphenolic wall costs.** Lignin and lignin-like polymers come from the phenylpropanoid
pathway: shikimate → phenylalanine → **PAL** → cinnamate → *p*-coumarate → C4H, 4CL, CCR, CAD →
monolignols → laccase/peroxidase polymerisation. That is an eight-plus enzyme pathway, and it is
characteristic of vascular plants. Evolving it *de novo* in a rhizarian — or in any lineage that did not
already have it — is a large, coordinated innovation.

**What a phenolic protein costs.** Enrich an existing structural protein in tyrosine, and oxidise. The
cross-linking is catalysed by **peroxidase, laccase or tyrosinase** — ordinary oxidative enzymes,
distributed across eukaryotes — and oxidative coupling between tyrosyl side chains occurs naturally.
The result is di-tyrosine and oligo-tyrosine cross-links.

This is the standard convergent solution to "make a tough insoluble structure," arrived at
independently in mussel byssus (DOPA-rich), insect sclerotin, plant extensins and the sea-urchin
fertilisation envelope. The change required is a shift in amino acid composition plus oxidative
cross-linking — not a new metabolic pathway.

**So the two hypotheses are not equally costly.** Under the polyphenol reading, an allogromiid-grade
ancestor must discard its protein–polysaccharide wall entirely and invent a novel structural polymer
class. Under the phenolic-protein reading, it keeps the wall it has, enriches it in tyrosine, and
cross-links it.

### And this is not merely permissive — it discriminates

The obvious objection is that easy-to-evolve chemistry helps every candidate equally, so it removes an
objection to H3′ without supporting it. That is *mostly* right, but not entirely, because the
compositional gap that remains differs by candidate:

| Ancestral wall | To reach a phenolic-protein wall, must lose | Size of step |
|---|---|---|
| **Allogromiid**: >60% protein, ~13% sugars | ~13% sugars; cross-link existing protein | **Small** |
| **Fungal**: chitin + β-glucan + glycoprotein | Its entire polysaccharide structural framework | **Large** |

Sugars are more labile than cross-linked protein, so an allogromiid needs to lose the smaller and more
losable component. A fungus would have to abandon chitin and β-glucan, which are its structural
mainstay.

So under the phenolic-protein reading the sugar-absence observation — the robust part of Loron et al.'s
chemistry — **still discriminates, and it discriminates less against an allogromiid than against a
fungus.** That is a real differential advantage, not just a removed obstacle.

### What it costs Loron et al.

Their positive claim suffers more than their negative one. "An extinct polyphenolic structural
biomacromolecule" and the inference to a previously undescribed lineage both lean on the wall polymer
being genuinely novel. If it was a tyrosine-cross-linked protein, the material is neither novel nor
lineage-diagnostic — many eukaryotes make such things — and the chemistry stops constraining affinity
except through the sugar absence.

## 4. What it has to survive: the amide deficit

The hard problem. A protein carries a backbone amide per residue, and *P. taiti* has the weakest amide
signature in the entire Rhynie assemblage (from the published matrix):

| | Amide II mean | Amide II median | Amide I median | N-products mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amoeba | 0.1796 | 0.2040 | 0.5241 | 0.2564 |
| Arthropods | 0.1739 | 0.2654 | 0.5566 | 0.2645 |
| Fungi | 0.1367 | 0.1357 | 0.5127 | 0.2300 |
| ***P. taiti*** | **0.0170** | **0.0040** | **0.2999** | **0.1333** |

The hypothesis therefore requires **selective loss of amide nitrogen while aromatic rings survived**.
That pathway is real — deamination is an ordinary diagenetic process and aromatics are more resistant —
and the authors concede the possibility explicitly (§1).

But it must clear the taphonomic control: co-occurring fungi and arthropods **kept** their amide signal
in the same chert. The hypothesis needs *P. taiti*'s protein to have followed a different diagenetic
path from theirs. A heavily quinone-tanned, di-tyrosine-cross-linked structural protein plausibly does
behave differently from ordinary cellular protein — but that is now a third auxiliary assumption
(protein wall + selective N loss + selective sugar loss), and each one costs the hypothesis content.

One point in its favour: *P. taiti* is **not** devoid of N-products (0.1333 mean), and 8 of the 24
fungi fall *below* its median on that feature. The nitrogen signal is low, not absent.

**But the closest available proxy runs hard the other way.** *Palaeoleptochlamys hassii* is a
proteinaceous, non-agglutinated, non-chitinous **protist test** preserved in the same chert — the best
existing analogue for "protein-walled protist through Rhynie diagenesis." It has the **highest mean
Amide II of any group in the dataset (0.1796)**, against *P. taiti*'s 0.0170. A tenfold gap between the
fossil and the nearest thing to what the hypothesis proposes.

Does sclerotisation rescue it? The hypothesis needs cross-linked protein to behave differently from
ordinary protein during diagenesis. The one sclerotised material in the dataset is **arthropod
cuticle** — and it has the *highest median* Amide II (0.2654). That looks fatal, but it is confounded:
arthropod cuticle is chitin plus protein, and chitin's N-acetyl groups contribute amide I and II
independently of any protein. So the arthropod amide signal may be chitin-dominated and is not a clean
test.

**Net: no clean test of the escape route exists in the dataset.** A non-chitinous sclerotised protein
in the same chert would settle it, and there isn't one. The route is not closed, but it is not
supported either — and the proteinaceous protist that *is* there sits at the opposite extreme from
*P. taiti*.

## 5. The decisive test has never been run

**Loron et al. performed no pyrolysis.** Their molecular work is ATR-FTIR, benchtop FTIR on macerates,
synchrotron micro-FTIR, and a targeted GC-MS biomarker search for perylene. The words "pyrolysis,"
"amino acid," "tryptophan," "tyrosine," "phenylalanine," "indole" and "pyrrole" appear **nowhere** in
the paper.

Py-GC-MS settles this question, because **nitrogen-bearing aromatic pyrolysis products are unambiguous
protein markers whereas phenols are not**:

| Pyrolysis product | Source | Diagnostic? |
|---|---|---|
| **Indole, methylindoles** | **Tryptophan** | **Yes — unambiguous** |
| Pyrrole | Proline / hydroxyproline | Yes |
| Phenylacetonitrile, cyanobenzenes | Phenylalanine | Yes |
| Diketopiperazines | Adjacent amino acid pairs | Yes |
| Toluene, styrene | Phenylalanine | Fairly |
| **Phenol, methylphenols** | **Tyrosine *or* a phenolic biopolymer** | **No — ambiguous** |

That last row is the crux, and Allen et al. (2000) state it exactly, from the opposite direction:

> "the observation of abundant phenol and methyl phenols **may not be specific for tyrosine** since
> these products may also originate from a **non-proteinaceous phenolic biopolymer**"

The ambiguity runs both ways. Allen et al. worried their foraminiferal phenols might be polyphenol;
this hypothesis asks whether *Prototaxites*'s phenols might be tyrosine. **The N-heterocycles break the
tie**, and Allen et al. detected that whole suite — indoles included — in foraminiferal material, so
the method works on samples of this kind.

### Four tests, in order

1. **Py-GC-MS.** Look for indole/methylindoles, pyrrole, nitriles, diketopiperazines. Their presence
   supports a protein wall; phenols without N-heterocycles supports a polyphenol. Never attempted.
2. **Thioacidolysis or DFRC.** These cleave **β-O-4 aryl ether linkages**, which true lignin has and a
   tyrosine-derived protein polymer does not. This is the cleanest structural discriminator between the
   two hypotheses. (Note Loron et al. *do* report ether bands in the macerate FTIR — a point for
   lignin, but non-specific.)
3. **Bulk C/N, δ¹⁵N, and N K-edge XANES.** Quantifies how much nitrogen survives and speciates it —
   amide vs heterocyclic vs absent. Still no elemental data exists for *Prototaxites* at all.
4. **The Wiemann framework.** Loron et al. cite McCoy et al. (2020) and Wiemann et al. on chemical
   signatures of fossil soft tissues (refs 45–46) but do not apply the method. It is built specifically
   around protein-derived fossil polymers — advanced glycoxidation/lipoxidation end products and
   N-heterocycles — and is the existing methodology closest to this question.

---

## 6. Verdict

**Not excluded, genuinely interesting, and unusually cheap to test.**

It is the best-aimed idea in this sequence: it targets the one inference that has survived every other
challenge, it explains an anomaly (H-type dominance) that the lignin reading does not, and the
technique that would settle it — pyrolysis GC-MS — has never been applied to this fossil despite being
standard for exactly this question and already used on foraminifera in 2000.

It carries a real cost: three auxiliary assumptions, and an amide deficit that is the most extreme in
the assemblage. If the pyrolysate turns out to contain indoles and diketopiperazines, much of the
argument in this repository needs rewriting. If it contains phenols and no nitrogen heterocycles, the
polyphenol reading is confirmed by a genuinely independent method and the chemical case against every
foraminiferal hypothesis hardens considerably.

Either result is worth having, which is the mark of a good hypothesis.
