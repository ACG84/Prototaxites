# Does any deep-sea monothalamid wall chemistry exist?

Short answer: **no.** Targeted searching turns up mineralogy, ultrastructure, morphology and molecular
phylogeny for deep-sea monothalamids, and no organic wall chemistry at all. The gap identified in
[`existing-analogues.md`](existing-analogues.md) §5b is real and total.

---

## 1. What does not exist

No FTIR, Raman, pyrolysis GC-MS, proteomic or quantitative compositional analysis of the organic wall
or cement of any bathyal or abyssal monothalamid. Nothing for *Bathysiphon*, *Rhizammina*, *Nodellum*,
*Tinogullmia*, *Toxisarcon*, *Resigella*, *Nemogullmia*, or any of the deep-sea clades where molecular
work reports the highest cryptic diversity.

Nothing for xenophyophore granellare either — confirmed for the third time in this repository.

### The instructive near-miss

**Kim et al., "The chemical composition of a new 'mica sandwich' foraminiferal species... *Capsammina
crassa* sp. nov."** — a paper with "chemical composition" in its title, on a monothalamid of a genus
otherwise known from the bathyal NE Atlantic.

It is **mineralogy**. ESEM-EDS identified 15 mineral types (quartz dominant, albite, biotite, calcite,
orthoclase, phengite, sericite…) and mapped Si, Al, S, Cl, Fe, I, Ti, Ca, Mg, K. **No XRD, FTIR or
Raman.** The organic component gets one descriptive sentence — "thin layers of organic material
organized as an elongated network" cementing grains to mica plates — and no characterisation at all.

That is the field's convention: for an agglutinated monothalamid, "chemical composition" means the
grains. The organic phase that actually bears on this argument is routinely not analysed.

## 2. What stands in for it: *Astrammina rara*

The nearest available taxon is not deep-sea — it is **Explorers Cove, Antarctica, shallow subtidal
under fast ice (~20–30 m)**. But it is a large, cold-water, organic-cemented monothalamid, and it is
the best-characterised monothalamid in existence, studied three independent ways:

| Study | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **DeLaca (1986)**, *J. Foram. Res.* 16(4):285–292 | Quantitative composition; **explicitly separated protoplasm from structural (test) organics** | Source of the ~19% lipid figure later questioned by Bowser & Bernhard as pseudopodial contamination. Full percentages not retrievable online. |
| **Bowser & Bernhard (1993)**, *J. Eukaryot. Microbiol.* 40:121–131 | Fine structure, elastic properties, distribution of the fibrous meshlike **bioadhesive** | Protein is an integral adhesive component — shown by **disruption of adhesive cables with disulfide reducing agents** |
| **Allen, Roberts & Murray (2000)** | FTIR + Py-GC-MS on isolated test and lining | Glycoproteinaceous, collagen-like; amide I at **1635 cm⁻¹** (β-sheet); proline–proline diketopiperazine in pyrolysate |

So the best-studied monothalamid, by three methods, is glycoproteinaceous.

### A specific point against the tyrosine route

Bowser & Bernhard's result identifies the cross-linking chemistry in *A. rara*'s bioadhesive as
**disulfide** — cysteine-based — since reducing agents disrupt the adhesive cables.

That is the **non-aromatic** cross-linking route. Di-tyrosine and disulfide are alternative strategies
for making an insoluble protein network, and the one monothalamid whose cross-link chemistry is known
uses the one that produces no aromatic signal.

This is n = 1 and it is a bioadhesive rather than a structural wall, so it does not refute the
aromatic-protein hypothesis in [`aromatic-protein-hypothesis.md`](aromatic-protein-hypothesis.md). But
it is the only direct evidence on the question, and it points the other way.

## 2b. The broader organic-walled grade: what exists

Widening from deep-sea taxa to organic-walled ("tectinous") foraminifera generally changes the picture
in two ways.

### There is older chemistry, and it is histochemical rather than spectroscopic

Hedley-era histochemistry on organic-walled forms used the Feulgen reaction (azure-A-Schiff), periodic
acid–Schiff, and naphthol yellow S to stain nuclei, polysaccharide and protein respectively. It
established a **protein and acid mucopolysaccharide** shell — and something more interesting than a
bulk composition:

> "a **three-layered test, with protein on the outside, polysaccharide on the inside**, and a
> Feulgen-positive layer in the middle"

So the organic test is **compositionally zoned**, not homogeneous. That matters for interpreting any
spectroscopic measurement: an ATR-FTIR spot samples a depth of a few micrometres and would weight the
outer, protein-rich layer. It also raises an obvious question nobody has asked — whether the zonation
varies enough between taxa to move the amide signal.

### There is real documented variation — but in ultrastructure, not composition

From comparative ultrastructural work on allogromiids:

> monothalamids "share a generally **fibrous ultrastructure** of the organic test or lining," but
> "significant differences in **layering, orientation of the fibrous fine structure, and the presence
> of electron-opaque outer layers** distinguish them," and simple organic walls "vary in their
> **mechanical and optical properties**."

That is genuine diversity across the organic-walled grade. It is architectural and mechanical, and it
has never been paired with compositional spectroscopy. *Cribrothalammina alba*, for instance, has a
finely fibrous inner organic lining beneath an agglutinated layer.

**Net: the broader organic-walled grade adds histochemistry (protein + acid mucopolysaccharide,
zoned) and ultrastructural variation, but no new spectroscopy.** Allen et al. (2000) remains the only
IR/pyrolysis work touching this grade.

## 2c. Correction: allogromiid size

[`allogromiid-hypothesis.md`](allogromiid-hypothesis.md) §3 states that organic-walled allogromiids are
"typically tens to hundreds of micrometres," citing an undescribed 30–60 µm species, and uses size as
an objection to H3′. **That understates the range.**

Organic-walled allogromiids "vary from **<50 micrometres to a centimetre or more** in length," and are
morphologically diverse — spherical, oval, sausage-shaped and thread-like, with one aperture
(*Allogromia*) or two terminal apertures (*Nemogullmia*, *Thiogullmia*).

So the gap to *P. taiti*'s 5.6 cm is roughly **5×**, not the two-to-three orders of magnitude implied.
That is a materially weaker objection than stated, and the size argument against H3′ should be
downgraded accordingly. It still holds against *P. loganii* at 8 m, and the morphological objections
(single chamber, container rather than skeleton, no tube systems or septa) are untouched.

## 3. Effect on the sampling argument

The count in [`existing-analogues.md`](existing-analogues.md) §5b does not improve. *A. rara* was
already among the ~10 characterised taxa; there is nothing new to add, and the deep-sea, giant and
non-marine monothalamids remain entirely unmeasured.

So the position is unchanged but now confirmed rather than assumed:

- **"Every foraminiferal organic wall anyone has measured is glycoproteinaceous"** — still the only
  defensible sentence.
- The measured set is ~10 taxa out of ~9,000–10,000 living species, and contains **no deep-sea
  monothalamid, no xenophyophore, no non-marine monothalamid, and no giant form**.
- Nobody has looked. That is now established by search rather than inferred.

---

## 4. What this makes worth doing

The experiment proposed in [`tectin-variants.md`](tectin-variants.md) §4 should target a **large,
cold-water or deep-sea monothalamid**, not *Allogromia*. The shallow culturable taxa are precisely the
ones already sampled, and re-measuring them adds nothing.

Material is obtainable: *Astrammina rara* has an established collection history at Explorers Cove and a
sequenced genome; deep-sea monothalamids are recovered routinely in abyssal sampling programmes, and
CCZ nodule surveys have yielded extensive xenophyophore collections.

The measurement itself is undemanding — ATR-FTIR on an isolated test, the same protocol Allen et al.
ran in 1997. The reason it has not been done is not difficulty. It is that nobody has had a reason to
ask.
